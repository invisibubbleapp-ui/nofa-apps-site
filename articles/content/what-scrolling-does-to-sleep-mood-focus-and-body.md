# What Hours of Scrolling Can Do to Your Sleep, Mood, Focus and Body (and What Actually Helps)

*This article is general information, not medical advice. If scrolling is affecting your sleep, mood, work or relationships, talk to a doctor or a mental-health professional.*

---

You open your phone to check one thing. Forty minutes later you are still there, thumb moving, and you could not say what you just watched. Most people have done this. This article looks at what research says about long scrolling sessions, where the evidence is strong, where it is thin, and what can help.

A note on wording first. "Doomscrolling" means scrolling through bad news or negative content over and over. "Mindless scrolling" means scrolling on autopilot, without choosing to continue. Neither is a medical diagnosis. They are names for behaviours, and researchers study how those behaviours relate to wellbeing.

## Why it is so hard to stop

Feeds are built without a natural ending. There is no last page, no final chapter, and the next item loads before you decide whether you want it. Researchers who study infinite scrolling describe a loop: you keep going because stopping requires a decision, and continuing does not.

That matters because most of us do not choose to lose an hour. We just never made the decision to stop.

## 1. Sleep

Sleep is one of the most studied areas. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of social media use, mental health and sleep found that social media use on its own was not associated with sleep problems or sleep duration. However, *problematic* social media use, meaning use that feels hard to control, was associated with sleep problems, although not with how long people slept. The review also noted a high level of variation between studies.

An earlier meta-analysis of 14 studies (2019) found positive associations between problematic smartphone use and poor sleep quality, depression and anxiety.

**What this means:** the link is with *how* you use your phone, not simply with owning one. Late-night scrolling you cannot stop is the pattern to watch.

## 2. Anxiety and low mood

The same 2024 review found small but significant associations between social media use and both depression and anxiety. Problematic use was associated with depression, anxiety and poorer wellbeing across platforms. It also found that social media use at the start of studies was associated with depression later on, but not the other way round, which points toward a direction of influence. Even so, the associations were small, and the researchers stress the variation between studies.

Doomscrolling has its own research:

- A 2026 cross-sectional study of 390 undergraduate students linked higher doomscrolling with higher anxiety, stress and depressive symptoms.
- A study reported by The Medical Republic found that doomscrolling predicted higher existential anxiety in two samples, with a weak-to-moderate effect.

Most of these studies are surveys. Surveys can show that two things go together, but they cannot prove that one causes the other. People who feel anxious may also scroll more. Results are not uniform across studies either.

**What this means:** heavy and hard-to-control use goes with worse mood and more anxiety on average, and doomscrolling in particular goes with more worry. That is not the same as saying scrolling causes a mental illness.

## 3. Attention, memory and "absent-mindedness"

Many people say that long scrolling leaves them scattered or forgetful. Researchers are studying how heavy use relates to attention and impulse control. Early reviews of brain-imaging studies report associations between problematic internet and smartphone use and measures related to cognitive control, but the authors say more research is needed to understand the mechanisms and whether use causes the changes.

**What this means:** this area is early. Be wary of headlines saying scrolling "damages your brain". The honest summary is that researchers see associations and are still working out what they mean.

## 4. Your eyes

The American Academy of Ophthalmology describes digital eye strain as a group of eye problems that can happen after staring at a screen for too long. Symptoms include blurry vision, headaches, and tired, dry eyes. One cause is that we blink less often when using screens.

A commonly recommended habit is the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds.

## 5. Neck, shoulders and posture

"Text neck" is the popular name for neck pain from looking down at a phone. Neck pain itself is common and real. But the evidence for text neck as a distinct condition is weaker than the headlines suggest. A scoping review of 41 articles found that the term is defined in different ways. Some researchers point out that many studies are small, use self-reported surveys, or have designs that cannot show cause and effect. Surveys do report that many heavy phone users have neck, shoulder or upper-back discomfort.

**What this means:** long sessions with your head bent may contribute to discomfort, but the science is not settled. Changing position and taking breaks is simple and costs nothing.

## 6. Sitting still for hours

Long scrolling is usually sedentary time. The World Health Organization notes that physical inactivity is one of the leading risk factors for death from noncommunicable diseases, and that higher amounts of sedentary behaviour are associated with poorer health outcomes.

Scrolling is not the only way to sit still. But an hour on the sofa that you did not plan is an hour you were not moving, and it adds up.

## 7. Time you meant to spend elsewhere

The effect people describe most often is the simplest. Writers on the subject say problems begin when use becomes hard to control and starts to crowd out sleep, work, study, relationships or physical activity.

Time is the one cost that every scroller can measure.

## Is it a "disorder"?

Words like "addiction" are used loosely. The situation is more careful than that:

- The World Health Organization's ICD-11 recognises **gaming disorder**.
- **Social media addiction** is not currently an independent ICD-11 diagnosis, and it is not formally recognised as a medical disorder, though it is widely studied under names such as "problematic social media use".
- **Doomscrolling** and **nomophobia** (fear of being without a phone) are names for behaviours researchers study, not diagnoses.

Numbers still show a real issue for young people. A World Health Organization survey of around 280,000 young people aged 11, 13 and 15 in 44 countries and regions found that 11% of adolescents showed signs of problematic social media use, up from 7% in 2018.

If your use feels out of control and is hurting your life, that is worth taking seriously whether or not it has an official label. A doctor or counsellor can help.

## What helps

None of this means you must quit your phone. It means choosing how long you scroll instead of letting the feed choose.

**1. Decide before you start.** Pick a limit while you are calm and not already in the feed. "Ten minutes" is easier to keep when you say it first.

**2. Make the stop visible.** A passive notification is easy to swipe away. A stop that interrupts what you are doing is harder to ignore. Researchers have studied design "friction", meaning small interruptions, as a way to reduce mindless scrolling while keeping people satisfied with the app.

**3. Use a tool, but expect modest results.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of digital self-control tools such as timers found a small-to-medium effect on reducing time spent on distracting technology, mostly in short-term studies, and called for longer research. Other research suggests that simply seeing a dashboard of your screen time raises awareness but does not necessarily make you feel more in control.

**4. Protect sleep.** Set a time after which the feed is off. Charge the phone away from your bed if you can.

**5. Move and rest your eyes.** Stand up between sessions and use the 20-20-20 rule.

**6. Notice how you feel afterwards.** If you finish a session feeling worse, that is useful information.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is doomscrolling?**
Doomscrolling is scrolling through bad news or negative content over and over, usually for longer than you meant to. It is a name for a behaviour, not a medical diagnosis.

**Is scrolling for hours bad for you?**
Research links problematic, hard-to-control use with worse sleep, more anxiety and low mood, and the time it takes from sleep and movement. The links are mostly associations, and effects differ from person to person. How you use your phone matters more than owning one.

**Is social media addiction a real disorder?**
Not as a formal diagnosis. Gaming disorder is recognised in ICD-11, but social media addiction is not currently an independent diagnosis, although researchers study problematic use widely.

**Does scrolling before bed affect sleep?**
Problematic social media use is associated with sleep problems in the research, although not with how long people sleep. Setting a cut-off time is a simple step.

**What is the fastest way to scroll less?**
Decide on a limit before you open the app, and use something that stops you when time is up, such as a scroll timer app like Invisibubble.
